♿ Disability Models — Compare Them Visually

Medical model
Focuses on the person's impairment or condition and treats disability primarily as an individual issue.
Social model
Focuses on barriers in society and the environment that disable people.
Charity model
Views people with disabilities as needing help or charity from others.
Economic model
Looks at disability in terms of economic consequences and participation.
Human-rights model
Emphasizes equality, dignity, participation and rights.
💡 Easy exam distinction Medical = person/condition. Social = barriers. Charity = help. Human rights = equality and rights.

🔄 From Impairment to Accessibility Barrier

Person
has an impairment or disability
→
Environment
may contain barriers
→
Challenge
task becomes difficult or impossible
→
Accessibility
remove or reduce the barrier
Visual example
A screen-reader user encounters an unlabeled button → the interface lacks an accessible name → adding an appropriate accessible name removes the barrier.
Motor example
A user cannot accurately activate a tiny target → increasing the target size provides an easier interaction.
Hearing example
A video contains important spoken information without captions → captions provide an equivalent way to access the information.

🛠️ Disability → Barrier → Assistive Technology

SituationPossible barrierExample AT / support
Blind or low-vision userVisual information is unavailable or difficult to perceiveScreen reader, magnification, refreshable braille display
Deaf or hard-of-hearing userAudio-only informationCaptions, transcripts, visual alerts
Motor disabilityPrecise physical interaction is difficultAlternative input devices, keyboard access, switch access
Cognitive disabilityComplex language, memory load or confusing navigationClear instructions, consistent navigation, supportive technologies
💡 RememberAssistive technology can help a person interact with an environment; accessibility also requires reducing barriers in the environment itself.

🏠 Accommodation vs Universal Design

Accommodation
Adjusts something for a particular person's needs.
VS
Universal Design
Designs the product/environment to work for as many people as possible from the start.
Accommodation example
Providing an individual alternative format because a particular person needs it.
Universal design example
Providing captions as part of the video experience so many people can use them when needed.

🎨 The 7 Principles of Universal Design

1. Equitable Use
Useful to people with diverse abilities
2. Flexibility in Use
Supports different preferences and abilities
3. Simple & Intuitive Use
Easy to understand
4. Perceptible Information
Information is communicated effectively
5. Tolerance for Error
Reduces hazards and consequences of mistakes
6. Low Physical Effort
Can be used efficiently and comfortably
7. Size & Space for Approach and Use
Enough space for different users and devices
💡 Quick recallEquitable → Flexible → Simple → Perceptible → Error-tolerant → Low effort → Space.

📚 UDL vs Universal Design vs Usability vs UX

Universal Design
Design of environments/products so they can be used by the widest range of people.
UDL
Applies flexible design principles to learning and teaching.
Usability
How effectively, efficiently and satisfactorily users can achieve goals.
UX
The broader experience a person has when interacting with a product or service.

🔎 Accessibility Example: Contrast & Resize

Contrast
Text and important visual information need sufficient contrast so users can distinguish them.
Resize text
Content should remain usable when text is enlarged, rather than becoming clipped or inaccessible.
Reflow
Content should adapt to a narrow viewport so users do not have to scroll in two dimensions unnecessarily for typical content.
Target size
Interactive targets should provide enough size and spacing to support users with motor and other interaction difficulties.

🧩 CPACC Question Strategy

1. Identify the concept
Is the question about a disability model, AT, universal design, WCAG, law, or strategy?
2. Look for the clue
Words such as barrier, rights, individual adjustment, testable requirement, or assistive technology often point to different concepts.
3. Eliminate distractors
Ask which answer actually addresses the situation rather than a related but different concept.
4. Apply, don't just memorize
Imagine the real user and the real barrier before selecting the answer.
